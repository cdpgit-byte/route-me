The setup

Four people, no tooling budget, and nobody who could code.

Garmentory is a marketplace for independent fashion boutiques. I ran partner acquisition there as a contractor, roughly ten hours a week, alongside my full-time role as an Account Manager at Uber. Business development was four people and effectively no tooling budget.

The tools that do this off the shelf cost more per seat than our entire stack cost to run. So the real choice was manual outreach, or building something.

I'm not an engineer. I'd never used Claude Code before this, and it was the first time I'd used AI models for anything beyond writing. Six weeks went into the build. It went live in May and ran from there.

The architecture

Six stages, run in order.

Each stage's job is to stop the next one from wasting money or emailing someone it shouldn't.

01

google-boutique-discovery.js

SerpApi prospecting that writes qualified boutiques into the pipeline. A balance check runs before every batch, and dry run is the default. Only --live writes.

02

stockist-scraper.js

Runs across an expanding brand list to surface boutiques already stocking labels that perform on the marketplace. Who a boutique already stocks tells you more than what category it sits in.

03

Airtable as system of record

Boutiques, contacts, activities and partners. Status rules keep anything at Live, Onboarding or Partner out of outreach entirely.

04

draft-outreach.js

Multi-touch sequencing with bounce handling, auto-response filtering and calendar guards. Alternate-email sweeps only fire when a known address has failed.

05

Live partner guard

Suppression runs against real partner status rather than a stale export, keeping 800+ active partners from ever receiving cold outreach.

06

orchestrator.js

Runs the phases in order so discovery, enrichment and drafting stay in sequence instead of racing each other into the same records.

What I learned building it

Anything that runs on its own needs brakes, and I had to decide where they went.

  • A balance check before any discovery run
  • Enrichment gated to one phase, never run across the whole table
  • Dry run as the default, --live as a deliberate act
  • Suppression against live partner status, never a stale export
  • A do-not-contact list that nothing is allowed to override

What broke

The parts that failed, and what I changed.

None of these are hypothetical. I hit every one of them in production, and the last one is the one I should have caught sooner.

Enrichment overwrote live contacts

It replaced addresses on threads that were already working, so now it fires at one phase only and never touches a record with activity on it.

Bounces failed silently

The parser missed Microsoft non-delivery reports entirely, so dead addresses sat in the pipeline looking perfectly healthy.

Auto-responses counted as interest

Out-of-office replies were logged as real responses, which inflated reply rates and pushed dead accounts forward in the sequence.

The data lied to me

One account logged replies that never happened. The system told me people were interested in us when nobody had written back.

I was pointed at a wall

More than half my pipeline sat on a platform we could not onboard, and none of it ever converted; the machine was working perfectly against accounts that could never close.

What it taught me about your market

Six weeks to build it. Then I had to keep it running.

Every provider I added was another integration to keep current. Every credit balance I checked by hand was spend I had no central view of. And the one model provider I committed to, I committed to because comparing alternatives was harder than staying put. I never evaluated anything.

That last one I wouldn't have understood from the outside. I was the single-provider customer. When I wanted something cheaper for the high-volume classification work, switching meant rewriting code I barely understood, so I didn't.

It's why, if I were building a list for OpenRouter, I'd start from companies that already built part of this themselves and are now paying to keep it running. I know what that costs.

The day job

I run a book of restaurant partners at Uber.

Account Manager, SMB Eats, in Seattle. Around eighty restaurant partners. The work is ads, promos, platform adoption and contract compliance, with Salesforce as the system of record.

The book I run this quarter does about $8M in annualized GMV. Starting in October, Uber is trusting me with roughly $40M — five times the territory, on the strength of how this quarter went. On the accounts I actively grow, 22% to 30% quarter over quarter, and I closed my six-week ramp at 101% of quota.

It is relevant here for one reason. Objection handling is not theory for me. I do it most weeks, with merchants who have every reason to say no and regularly do.

What transfers

Not the architecture.

OpenRouter's motion is nothing like a boutique marketplace. Arriving with this system and assuming it maps would be the mistake.

What transfers is the loop. Notice friction, build something, watch it break, fix it, then check whether it actually moved anything. That part works on a motion I've never seen before.

So before building anything, I'd want to know:

  • Where the AE team feels the handoff breaks today
  • Which signals are already tracked internally, so I'm not rebuilding something that exists
  • What a good meeting looks like measured at the opportunity stage, not the booking stage

Someone without an engineering background built a system the business depended on, and kept it running. That is the part I would take from this.

Why change now

I put the build work down on purpose. That is how I knew I wanted it.

I wound down the Garmentory contract at the end of September to clear room for the bigger book at Uber. That was the right call for the job I have, and the work there is going well, which is why the book is growing in the first place.

It also meant choosing account management over systems building, deliberately, with both on the table. Making that choice is what told me which one I actually wanted. The ten hours a week I was spending on the harder problem is the work I would rather do full time, and this is me looking for the version of it that is a real job.
