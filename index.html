Garmentory · contract, ten hours a week

First, what Garmentory is.

Garmentory is an online marketplace where independent fashion boutiques and emerging designers sell to shoppers in one place. About 800 boutiques and brands sell on it. My job was finding the next boutiques and getting them live, as one of four people on business development.

I did it as a contractor alongside my full-time job at Uber, and the first six weeks went into building the system below. From April to August 2026 I signed ten boutiques. The platform published 66 other new storefronts in the same window. My ten produced twice the sales of the other 66 combined, and every one of them sold. Hollow markers show the comparison point.

What broke

I hit every one of these in production. The last one I should have caught sooner.

Enrichment overwrote live contacts

It replaced addresses on threads that were already working. Now it fires at one stage only and never touches a record with activity on it.

Bounces failed silently

The parser missed Microsoft non-delivery reports, so dead addresses sat in the pipeline looking healthy.

Auto-replies counted as interest

Out-of-office replies were logged as real responses. That inflated reply rates and pushed dead accounts forward.

The data lied to me

One account logged replies that never happened. The system said people were interested when nobody had written back.

I was pointed at a wall

More than half my pipeline sat on a platform we could not onboard, and none of it converted. The machine worked perfectly against accounts that could never close.

What it taught me about your market

I was the single-provider customer.

Every provider I added was another integration to keep current. Every credit balance I checked by hand was spend I had no central view of. I committed to one model provider because comparing alternatives was harder than staying put. When I wanted something cheaper for the high-volume classification work, switching meant rewriting code I barely understood, so I didn't.

It's why I'd start your list from companies that already built part of this themselves and are now paying to keep it running. I know what that costs.

How I'd build your list

Start from the problem you solve, then look for public evidence of it.

A filter finds companies that match a description. A signal finds companies that have the problem. This is how I'd approach it.

AAn account has to clear four layers
01

Workload exists

Is AI running in a live product? Shipped features, models named in docs, a customer-facing assistant.

02

Growing

Is usage expanding? New AI features, a wider rollout, a new AI team or leader.

03

Complexity

More than one provider or model? Several providers documented, or users can pick models.

04

Reason to care now

What changed recently? A launch, a model-selection rollout, a platform initiative, an ownership change.

Layer 4 separates a real prospect from a long list of companies that use AI. A single-provider commitment is a buying trigger, not a disqualifier.
